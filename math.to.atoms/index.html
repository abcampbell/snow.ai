How we get these numbers

Think of $100 of spending. Better math helps us improve $25 of it. The new technology gets used on half of that: $12.50. Cut that cost by 40%, and we save $5 out of the original $100.

We apply the same 5% reduction to the costs, lost healthy years, emissions and project times in this example. That shared percentage is an assumption you can change.

By 2046, this gives $1.81T a year in savings and avoided losses, including $600B from energy. Health is counted separately: about 140 million healthy years of life saved around the world each year. These are annual amounts at the end of the twenty years, not amounts received every year from the start.

Energy costs 5% less. We avoid 1.92 billion tonnes of CO₂ from energy each year. A project that would take ten years takes nine and a half.

What changes the answer?

  • Help with more problems. If math helps us improve half of the area instead of a quarter, annual savings double to $3.62T. The other benefits double too.
  • Get the solutions used. If only a quarter gets used instead of half, the benefits halve. They also halve if the improvement is 20% rather than 40%.
  • Take longer to build. Spread the same change over forty years and the full annual benefit arrives in 2066. It builds half as fast.

A better turbulence model, for example, could help us design an aircraft that burns less fuel. The saving arrives when that aircraft is built and flown. A better proof alone does not save the fuel.

The map helps you choose which math problems look most useful, taking account of how hard the math and engineering are. It cannot yet tell us how much new math will produce a particular saving. You choose that step in the assumptions; the charts show what would follow if it happens.
