How the GDP gap is calculated

$600B/yr energy saving ÷ $120T reference GDP × 1.5 capital response = +0.75% at full deployment. The chart applies that percentage to the GDP trend, phased in from 2026. Current trend continues the recent historical growth rate.

The dollar benchmark is $900B/yr on the fixed reference economy. The curves use 2021 purchasing-power dollars: applying a percentage to future GDP is not a currency conversion. Health value and the other savings are not added to these GDP curves.

This assumes real productivity gains. Price transfers, transition costs and other sectors need separate estimates. The capital response follows an IMF electricity study; this global application and its rollout time are assumptions.

What is better math worth?

Better math can lead to better machines, treatments and decisions. The payoff comes when we build them and put them to use.

For example, a 20% cut in the $12T energy bill saves $2.4T a year: 2% of the $120T GDP reference. That measures a smaller bill; the conditional energy GDP benchmark is shown above.

Cheaper energy could let us make more with the same workers and machines. To estimate how much, we need to know:

  • What physically gets saved. A cheaper price can shift income from sellers to buyers. Using fewer real resources for the same output is a different gain.
  • What gets built next. How much new production the freed resources support, what new products become possible, and what the transition costs.
  • What happens as use grows. More demand can raise output while using up some of the energy savings. Spending and population are held fixed here.

GDP counts value added, rather than adding every supplier's sales together. Lower prices can also reduce money GDP even when the amount produced rises.

How we get these numbers

Start with $100 of spending. Math helps us improve $25 of it. The new solutions get used on $12.50. Cut those costs by 40%, and we save $5 out of the original $100.

This example uses the same 5% change for costs, lost healthy years, emissions and project times. The starting spending and health figures stay fixed. You choose how much useful improvement the math makes; the model shows what follows.

By 2046, that gives $1.81T a year in savings and avoided losses, including $600B from energy. Separately, we save 140M healthy years of life each year and avoid 1.92 billion tonnes of CO₂ from energy. A ten-year project takes 9.5 years.

Energy and other production costs are assumed to be separate; overlap still needs checking. Health is priced in the combined total above. GDP history uses 2021 purchasing-power dollars; savings use fixed reference spending, compared with an assumed $120T world economy. Energy history is an oil-price proxy, and delivery history is US output per hour. Neither is a global measure of all energy costs or project delivery times.

What changes the answer?

  • Help with more problems. Double the share that math helps with, and the benefits double.
  • Get the solutions used. Halve the share that gets used, or halve how much better it is, and the benefits halve.
  • Take longer to build. Double the rollout time and the same full annual benefit takes twice as long to arrive. It builds half as fast.

A better turbulence model, for example, could help us design an aircraft that burns less fuel. The saving arrives when that aircraft is built and flown. A better proof alone does not save the fuel.

The map helps you choose which math problems look most useful, taking account of how hard the math and engineering are. It cannot yet tell us how much new math will produce a particular saving. You choose that step in the assumptions; the charts show what would follow if it happens.
