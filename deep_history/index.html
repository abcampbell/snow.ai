4,400 YEARS OF INTEREST RATES

The longest documented financial series in existence. Stitched from Mesopotamian clay tablet loan records (2400 BC) through Greek temple deposits, Roman lending laws, Venetian prestiti, Florentine Monte, Dutch Republic government bonds, the Bank of England (1694), and consols.

Secular trend
Rates halve every ~700 years

Log-linear decline from Sumerian 20-33% to modern 1-3%. The Early Medieval spike to 75% reflects the collapse of financial institutions after Rome — risk premium, not policy. Rate spikes lead state conflict by ~11 years (r = 0.27).

GRAIN PRICES + VOLCANIC ERUPTIONS

Staple grain prices in silver-equivalent from the Babylonian Astronomical Diaries (384 BC) through medieval European wheat to 1954. Below: volcanic eruption intensity (VEI). The physical channel is clear — eruptions cause volcanic winters that kill harvests.

Volcanic → Grain (Europe + China simultaneously)
r = 0.40 – 0.53

The strongest cross-civilization signal in the dataset. Eruptions spike grain prices on both sides of Eurasia at the same time. The physical forcing channel is real and transcontinental.

THE GREAT DIVERGENCE

When did Europe pull ahead of Asia in living standards? Daily wages in grams of silver for unskilled laborers — Beijing, Amsterdam, London, and Istanbul — with 30-year rolling means to reveal the structural trend.

Beijing / London wage ratio
0.39 (1550s) → 0.11 (1800s)

The gap opened gradually after 1600, not suddenly. Amsterdam was richer than London until ~1700. Istanbul near parity with London in the 1450s. London wages correlate with New World silver inflows (r = 0.64) and Sound Toll trade volume (r = 0.53). The divergence is entangled with Atlantic trade expansion.

ROMAN DEBASEMENT → INFLATION

The denarius lost 97% of its silver content over 500 years — from 97% fine (Republic) to 2.5% (Gallienus, 268 AD). Shown alongside the English penny's silver content for a millennium of comparison. One of the clearest debasement-to-inflation episodes in history.

Two great debasements
Rome 97→2.5% · England 92→8%

Nero's clipping (64 AD) started gradual decline; Caracalla (215) and Gallienus (268) accelerated collapse. The English penny followed a slower arc from 925 fineness (760 AD) to the Great Debasement under Henry VIII. Both ended with fiscal crisis and monetary reform.

BALTIC TRADE vs ENGLISH WAGES

2.15 million ship passages through the Danish Sound (1497–1857) vs English real wages. Standardized and smoothed.

Co-movement
r = 0.50 in levels, 0.20 first-difference

Strong co-movement but largely a shared upward trend — they co-evolved with Early Modern growth. No strong directional causation. Baltic grain supply does inversely correlate with European wheat prices though — supply-side causation in the grain market is real.

SOVEREIGN BOND YIELDS — 4 COUNTRIES

Government borrowing costs from Dutch Republic bonds (1600), French rentes (1522), British consols (1688), and US treasuries (1790). The correct Dutch series — dutch_republic_govt.pct_yield — shows the Netherlands as the lowest-cost sovereign borrower in Early Modern Europe.

Financial revolution sequence
Netherlands → England → France → USA

The Netherlands led the financial revolution — lowest rates in Europe from 1600, achieving 2-3% yields when France was at 8-10%. Britain converged after the Glorious Revolution (1688). France was persistently higher — a political risk premium reflecting institutional fragility. The US started high and converged after the Civil War.

UK GDP PER CAPITA — 930 YEARS

The longest continuous GDP estimate for any country. Bank of England Millennium dataset, 1086–2016.

Flat for 700 years. Then exponential. The Industrial Revolution is the most dramatic structural break in the entire dataset — nothing in the preceding seven centuries predicted it. The Black Death (1348) actually raised per-capita income by killing a third of the population.

Imperial Records, Disasters & Prices

Four new charts drawing on Chinese Qing Dynasty Shilu (實錄) entries, provincial grain price reports across 20 provinces, drought and flood event counts from reaches data (1368–1911), and Korean Joseon-era commodity prices and wages from the Global Price & Income History Group.

DROUGHT & FLOOD vs GRAIN PRICES — CHINA (1368–1911)

Red bars show drought event counts; blue bars (inverted) show flood events. The gold line tracks the mean Qing grain price across 20 provinces in taels per shi. The Ming–Qing transition (1644) marks a structural break in disaster frequency.

Disaster–price channel
544 years of drought + flood data

Drought events cluster in the late 18th and 19th centuries — coinciding with the Qing fiscal crisis and the Taiping Rebellion. Grain prices across 20 provinces track disaster intensity with visible lag. The physical forcing channel operates at the sub-provincial level: Zhili (直隸) and Shandong (山東) show the strongest drought-price correlations.

IMPERIAL RECORDS: SHILU ENTRIES & DISASTERS

Annual count of Qing Shilu (實錄) entries — the official daily records of the emperors — alongside stacked drought and flood events. Administrative capacity tracks with disaster documentation.

Imperial bureaucracy as sensor network
557 years of Shilu records

Shilu entry counts rise dramatically in the 18th century (Kangxi–Qianlong peak), reflecting bureaucratic expansion. The late Qing decline in entries mirrors the dynasty's administrative collapse. Disaster documentation scales with state capacity — more entries doesn't necessarily mean more disasters; it means better detection.

KOREA (JOSEON) — PRICES & WAGES

Commodity prices and daily wages from the Joseon Dynasty (1392–1910), drawn from the Global Price & Income History Group. Rice prices in copper/silver equivalents, cotton cloth, wheat, and laborer wages.

Joseon economy
195 years of rice price data

Korean rice prices show structural inflation from the mid-18th century — the same period as Qing China's grain price rise. Copper wages plateau while silver wages decline, reflecting the global silver deflation that hit both East Asian economies. The Imjin War (1592) and Manchu Invasion (1636) mark exogenous shocks visible in the price record.

CHINA vs KOREA — PARALLEL EMPIRES

Chinese Shilu entry counts (top) vs Korean rice prices and copper wages (bottom). Two East Asian empires, two documentary traditions, one shared monetary and climate system.

Cross-regional synchrony
Ming → Qing · Joseon → Opening

The Imjin War (1592) and Ming–Qing transition (1644) appear as shocks in both records. The 18th-century peak in Chinese administrative output coincides with Joseon's price inflation — both driven by silver flows and demographic pressure. The Taiping Rebellion (1850s) and Korea's forced opening (1876) mark the beginning of the end for both traditional orders.

GOLD / SILVER RATIO ACROSS MILLENNIA

Stable at ~12-15:1 for two thousand years (the classical bimetallic ratio). Then silver collapsed after 1870 (demonetization, industrial production). The ratio hit 86:1 by 2020. The 2,500-year stability is remarkable — a physical constant of monetary systems until industrial mining broke it.

NILE FLOODS → CHINESE DROUGHTS

A teleconnection across 5,000 miles. High Nile floods in Egypt predict drought events in China 5 years later.

Mechanism
r = 0.34 at 5-year lag

El Niño strengthens the Indian Ocean monsoon (more Nile flooding from Ethiopian highlands) while disrupting the East Asian monsoon (more Chinese droughts). The direction is physically plausible and the lag structure suggests low-frequency climate oscillation.

THE FOURTH TURNING — TESTED

Strauss & Howe claim an ~80-year cycle of crisis in Anglo-American history. We tested their sine-wave prediction against independently-compiled crisis intensity.

Verdict
r = 0.021 — ZERO CORRELATION

The predicted cycle has no relationship with actual crises. The "Awakening" phase is actually more crisis-intensive than the "Crisis" phase (18.3 vs 16.4, p = 0.15). War clustering in "Crisis" turnings (79% vs 62%) is circular — they drew the boxes around wars they already knew about. The real cycles are solar (~11yr, ~60yr, ~200yr), not generational (~80yr).

SYNTHESIS
Climate drives economics drives politics

The dominant signal across 4,600 years of data is physical forcing through agriculture. Solar minima → cooling → harvest failure → grain price spike → real wage collapse → political instability → war. The chain takes 5-15 years. It works across civilizations simultaneously — volcanic eruptions spike grain prices on both sides of Eurasia.

The Great Divergence was gradual (post-1600), entangled with Atlantic trade. The Price Revolution hammered real wages 45%. Interest rates show a 4,400-year secular decline. The Fourth Turning is noise.

The East Asian evidence — 557 years of Chinese Shilu records, 20-province Qing grain prices, and 195 years of Korean Joseon commodity data — confirms the same pattern: drought clusters → grain spikes → fiscal crisis → dynastic collapse. The Ming–Qing and Joseon transitions are visible in every series.
